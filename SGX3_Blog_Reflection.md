# From Sensors to Scientific Computing: Reflections on FacultyHack@Gateways 2026

Participating in FacultyHack@Gateways 2026 gave me the opportunity to rethink how I can introduce advanced computing concepts in an entry-level IoT course. My curriculum project, **BRIDGE-CI: From Sensors to Scientific Computing**, expands LAN 120: IoT Fundamentals by helping students move from building Arduino-based sensor systems to analyzing data in Jupyter notebooks, visualizing results, identifying patterns or anomalies, and connecting their findings to cybersecurity and critical infrastructure.

One of my goals is to help students see that the investigative process they use with a small sensor project can scale to much larger scientific computing environments. The emphasis is not on turning beginning students into Python or HPC experts, but on helping them become comfortable with the tools, workflows, and possibilities available to them.

My hands-on work with HPC tools is still continuing. I have accounts in place, appointments scheduled, and I am preparing to request the computing allocations needed to continue developing and testing the student experience. FacultyHack gave me a much clearer understanding of the resources available and helped me identify a practical path for bringing them into the classroom.

A major highlight of the experience was meeting the other FacultyHack participants and mentors. The conversations, ideas, and feedback were incredibly valuable, and I also met many people throughout the Gateways conference with whom I expect to continue collaborating. Those relationships may end up being one of the most meaningful outcomes of the experience.

I was also grateful for the opportunity to present both my poster and my paper during the conference. Those were experiences I likely would not have had without the FacultyHack opportunity, and they gave me the chance to share my work, receive feedback, and participate more fully in the Science Gateways community.

My biggest takeaway from Gateways 2026 is that advanced computing can be made approachable. With the right scaffolding, tools, and support, community college students can begin engaging with scientific computing much earlier than we sometimes assume.

I plan to continue working with these rich resources because they can help reduce barriers for community college students and expand access to computing research, HPC, and Science Gateway resources. One of the most valuable outcomes of FacultyHack was seeing how these tools can be introduced in ways that are approachable, relevant, and connected to the work students are already doing. FacultyHack helped me see new possibilities for my course while also connecting me with a community that can help make those possibilities real.
